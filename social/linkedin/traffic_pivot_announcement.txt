Why we're changing the next phase of Cooperative Traffic Research.

The question has never been whether we can make a traffic simulator produce a favorable result.

It's whether voluntary coordination among drivers can improve overall traffic compared with equally informed independent decisions, without imposing unacceptable costs on people who never agreed to participate.

Our latest development experiment gave us a reason to rethink how we test that question.

In a synthetic network of 1,000 drivers, just 12 changed their departure times by 10 seconds. In a revised comparison that held each driver's speed factor constant, the coordinated schedule produced 2,131 additional network-seconds of travel cost relative to independent decisions.

The 988 drivers who didn't change their departures experienced a combined 2,177 additional seconds.

But the investigation revealed something equally important about our experimental environment.

Our original comparison had inadvertently assigned different driving characteristics to 380 vehicles. We corrected that. Further inspection of SUMO's source code revealed that geographically distant lanes can share random-number streams used in driving behavior.

That creates a plausible source of simulation-wide interactions that cannot yet be separated quantitatively from physical traffic propagation.

So we're changing direction.

Rather than build another forecasting model or keep adjusting one simulator, our next research milestone is to evaluate independently developed traffic simulation frameworks.

SUMO remains a valuable reference. The objective is to determine whether the phenomena we're observing persist under different models of traffic behavior—and whether the conditions for a fair comparison can be maintained.

We're not announcing a breakthrough in cooperative traffic.

We're documenting what it will take to establish one credibly.
